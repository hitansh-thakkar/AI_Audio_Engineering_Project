## 1. How the errors are calculated (and what was wrong before)

### WER and CER

Both are **pooled** over clips: (sum of substitutions + deletions + insertions) / (sum of
reference words or characters). A mean of per-clip rates would let a 3-word clip weigh as much as
a 60-word one. CER is computed on text with whitespace removed, so it measures spelling and not
word segmentation. WER can exceed 100% because insertions are unbounded (a looping model inserts
hundreds of words).

Before scoring, reference and hypothesis go through the same normaliser (`src/evaluation/normalize.py`):

1. Unicode NFC.
2. indic-nlp per-language canonicalisation (chandrabindu/anusvara variants, two-part vowel signs).
3. casefold (only affects Latin script).
4. Unicode punctuation and symbols (P\*, S\*, including danda) become spaces.
5. Format characters (ZWJ/ZWNJ) are dropped.
6. Combining marks (matras, virama) are **never** removed.

Whisper's built-in `BasicTextNormalizer` deletes every combining mark, so "किताब" becomes "कतब".
That inflates Indic WER when only one side is normalised, and it hides real vowel errors when
both sides are. Both teammates used this repository's scorer, and re-scoring their raw
transcripts independently reproduces their numbers:

| System | Self-reported WER (833 / excl. Sindhi) | Re-scored here (833 / excl. Sindhi) |
|---|---|---|
| Sarvam | 25.35% / 24.92% | 25.33% / 24.90% |
| faster-whisper (model unrecorded) | 90.32% / 88.59% | 90.32% / 88.59% |

Sarvam's differ by 0.02 pp (immaterial; a few clips normalise slightly differently) and
faster-whisper's match exactly. None of the 833 reference texts in either zip differed from
our manifest (`reference_text_mismatches: 0`), so both teams scored against the same ground truth
and the error calculation is not the reason faster-whisper looks bad.

### Pitfalls found and fixed while scoring

- **Sindhi is excluded from headline numbers.** References are Devanagari (as IndicVoices and
  Nirantar ship them); ASR systems write Sindhi in Perso-Arabic (48% of faster-whisper's Sindhi
  outputs were the wrong script), so every word is an error regardless of what was heard. Sindhi
  is still reported separately in `data/*.json` (`overall_all`).
- **Script check.** A non-code-switch hypothesis is flagged "wrong script" when fewer than 50% of
  its letters are in the expected script. This separates "did not understand" from "wrote it in
  another alphabet".
- **Missing clips count as total deletion.** A system cannot improve its score by failing on
  hard clips. Earlier the Gemini ASR runner cached an *empty* transcript when the API returned a
  quota error, which would have silently scored quota failures as deletions *and* never retried
  them. Failures are now not cached and are retried on resume.
- **MMS Sanskrit.** MMS-1B-all has no `san` adapter; without a fallback every Sanskrit clip
  scored 100%. It now uses the Hindi (Devanagari) adapter, scoring 93.7% — still very poor, and
  flagged.
- **IndicWav2Vec decoding.** Its repository's default processor needs a KenLM language model.
  Greedy CTC is used instead so it is compared like-for-like with MMS, which is also greedy.

### DER

`pyannote.metrics` DiarizationErrorRate with overlapped speech scored and **collar 0** (the
strictest setting); collar 0.25 s is in `data/diar_bench.json`. DER = (missed speech + false
alarm + speaker confusion) / total reference speech. The hypothesis-to-reference speaker mapping
is the optimal (Hungarian) one, so label names do not matter. JER is also reported.

### Separation

SI-SNR improvement (dB) with permutation-invariant assignment, on 24 synthetic two-speaker
mixtures whose clean sources are known. "Overlap-region" scores only the overlapped span, which is
what the pipeline actually separates. "Assigned" scores the stream the pipeline *attributes* to
each diarized speaker, which also tests whether separated streams are put on the right speaker.

### LLM (reference-free)

There are no gold summaries, so each metric compares output with its own input transcript:

- **Rewrite CER:** CER of the cleaned dialogue against the input. A high value means the model
  rewrote speech instead of cleaning it.
- **Language accuracy:** the per-segment language label is correct.
- **Translation rate:** non-English segments that got an English translation.
- **Document grounding:** cross-lingual LaBSE cosine between the whole summary and the whole
  transcript. A summary counts as grounded if that cosine beats the 95th percentile of
  *mismatched* pairs. **Retrieval@1** is the share of summaries whose closest transcript is their own.
- **Novel numbers:** numbers in the summary or tags that never occur in the input. This is a
  fabrication proxy; digits are compared by value, so Devanagari "२" equals "2".

Two of these metrics were wrong in the first version, and both were fixed:

1. **Sentence-level grounding.** The first version scored each summary sentence against its best
   input segment. It barely tells a summary from a random transcript (AUC 0.73), because one
   summary sentence fuses several turns. Its "59% ungrounded sentences" was an artefact.
   Document-level grounding separates them with AUC 0.94, so it became the headline.
2. **Speaker labels as numbers.** The novel-number proxy counted "Speaker 2" as an invented
   number. Speaker labels are now excluded, and Gemini's score went from non-zero to 0.0.

## 2. Preprocessing

Every front-end variant was scored by **what it does to downstream ASR** (MMS, fixed), on 60
IndicVoices clips plus the Vaani clips (noisy). A front-end that "sounds cleaner" but raises WER
is worse.

{{preproc}}

![Preprocessing](charts/cmp_preprocessing.png)

**What was tested.** Seven front-ends on the same 258 clips: raw audio; peak + trim (the
pipeline default); a 70 Hz high-pass; LUFS −23; the project's spectral gate; `noisereduce`
stationary gating; and high-pass + noisereduce + LUFS. Each front-end feeds MMS-1B-all, with the
oracle language and the Indic-safe normaliser. Preprocessing is judged only by downstream WER.
SNR or listening tests reward denoisers that sound cleaner while erasing the cues a recogniser uses.

**Result.** Nothing beats raw audio, and the only large effect is negative.

- *Gain and filtering are neutral* (−0.0 to +0.25 pp). Wav2vec2-family models normalise every input
  waveform to zero mean and unit variance, so peak or LUFS scaling is erased before the first layer.
  A 70 Hz high-pass removes energy below the speech band, which the model already ignores. Trim
  changes only leading and trailing silence. On IndicVoices it helps slightly (59.0 → 58.5%), and on
  Vaani it hurts slightly (62.9 → 63.2%), probably by clipping soft word onsets.
- *The project spectral gate is close to neutral* (+0.25 pp). It is mild by design.
- *`noisereduce` is harmful* (+8.7 pp WER, +7.9 pp CER). Its stationary spectral gating suppresses
  low-energy consonants and fricatives along with the noise, and it leaves musical-noise artefacts.
  Neither occurs in the model's training data. It hurts most on Vaani (+9.1 pp), the noisy corpus
  it was supposed to rescue. The model is already robust to the real noise and not to the artefacts.
  Adding a high-pass and LUFS on top changes nothing (+8.9 pp).

**Decision.** Keep `normalize: peak`, `trim_silence: true` and `denoise: none`. Peak
normalisation is kept because it protects the diarization and separation stages from clipping,
not because it helps ASR. This matches the existing config comment ("distorts embeddings; prefer
denoise_output") and gives that comment measured evidence on the ASR side too.

**Caveat.** The 0.15–0.25 pp differences are below what 258 clips can resolve. Only the
noisereduce effect is clearly real: it shows up on both corpora, and on CER as well as WER.

Why ECAPA + spectral wins, and what the numbers do and do not show:

- **ECAPA beats MFCC once the speaker count matters.** With the true number of speakers given
  (oracle K), ECAPA embeddings cut sequential DER from 16.5% to 10.2%. With K estimated, ECAPA is
  right on 60% of sequential clips versus 20% for MFCC, because MFCC statistics capture channel
  and phoneme content as much as voice. On code-switch clips all local systems are within 2 pp,
  because those clips are easy to split (see below).
- **Spectral beats GMM** for both embeddings and both K settings. The GMM fits full covariances
  on a few dozen windows per clip, which overfits; spectral clustering only needs the affinity
  structure. Both clusterers are the proposal-required "hand-applied" methods.
- **Both pyannote systems under-count speakers.** They output one speaker on 22–23 of the 24
  code-switch clips. Each code-switch clip stitches a Hindi and an English segment, and the median
  ECAPA cosine between the two halves is {{h2_cs}} — *lower* than between known different speakers
  in the sequential set ({{h2_seq}}). They really are different voices, so assigning one speaker
  is a genuine error. Precision-2 is a paid cloud service; it is now an optional toggle, not the
  default.
- **The code-switch DER is mostly the reference, not the systems.** {{h1}} of the reference
  speaker-time on code-switch clips is silence by energy VAD, because each stitched clip's whole

## 6. Why the faster-whisper teammate's run fails

![FW error causes](charts/cmp_fw_error_causes.png)

{{fw_causes}}

A **repetition loop** here is an output of at least 8 words in which some word trigram occurs at
least 4 times. A **wrong script** output has fewer than 50% of its letters in the target script
(by language: {{fw_wrong_script_lang}}).

Three separate failures stack up:

1. **Whisper is weak on most Indic languages, even on clean output.** On the clips with no loop
   and the right script, it still scores far worse than Sarvam on the *same* clips:

   {{fw_clean}}

   Whisper's training data contains very little Assamese, Odia, Punjabi, Dravidian or Nepali
   speech, so this is the floor for any Whisper-family model without Indic fine-tuning.
   English (7.6%) and Urdu (19.1%), which Whisper saw a lot of, are fine.
2. **Repetition loops add hundreds of inserted words.** The loops are already in the raw Whisper
   segments, not added by post-processing: 106 of the 111 looped texts loop in the raw output.
   The run used temperature 0 with no fallback, no VAD and no previous-text conditioning. That
   decoding setup lets a low-confidence segment repeat itself until it reaches the maximum length.
3. **Wrong script, mostly Assamese.** Whisper has no Assamese tokenizer language, so it writes
   Assamese speech as Bengali or Devanagari.

**Which model was it?** The zip does not record it, so it is labelled "model unrecorded". The
reported model-load time was 0.6 s. Replaying the teammate's 20 worst looped clips here with
large-v3 gave these results:

| Replay (large-v3 unless noted) | WER on 20 looped clips | Clips still looping |
|---|---|---|
| teammate's original output | 191.3% | 20 |
| T = 0, no VAD (teammate's settings) | 104.8% | 1 |
| temperature fallback, no VAD | 99.4% | 1 |
| temperature fallback + VAD | 112.3% | 0 |
| batched, batch 8 | 94.9% | 1 |
| **small**, batched | 122.5% | 5 of 6 tested |

large-v3 does not reproduce the loop rate under any setting, while `small` does. A second
fingerprint pass replayed the teammate's exact settings with each checkpoint on 12 clips (6 looped,
6 normal). It measured character similarity to the teammate's output and counted reproduced loops:

| Checkpoint | Similarity, normal clips | Similarity, looped clips | Loops reproduced |
|---|---|---|---|
| tiny | 0.08 | 0.08 | 1 of 6 |
| base | 0.13 | 0.08 | 3 of 6 |
| small | **0.45** | **0.21** | **4 of 6** |

The run was cut off before medium finished. Among the checkpoints tested, `small` matches the
teammate's output best and reproduces most of the loops. The 0.6 s model-load time also points to
a small checkpoint. The likely explanation is that the run used a small Whisper
model with loop-prone decoding, on languages Whisper barely covers. Fixing the decoding
(temperature fallback, VAD, `condition_on_previous_text`) removes most loops but leaves the
underlying ~70% same-script WER. The real fix is an Indic-trained model (Sarvam, MMS, IndicConformer).

## 7. LLM post-processing

{{llm}}

All four backends wrote their summaries in English, so the grounding numbers are comparable.

- **Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite is the best at the stage's main job**, which is cleaning the dialogue
  without changing it. It returned a valid report for 34 of 34 inputs. It rewrote only 2.2% of
  characters, the lowest of any backend that actually translates. It labelled 97% of segments
  with the right language and invented 0 numbers. It is also the fastest, at a median of 11 s.
- **Qwen2.5-Coder 7B (local, Ollama) is the best fully-offline option.** Its summaries are the
  most specific of the four: 82% beat the mismatched-pair threshold and 79% retrieve their own
  transcript, against 74% and 68% for Gemini. It also invented 0 numbers. Its weaknesses are a
  rewrite CER of 11.4% (five times Gemini's), 86% language-label accuracy, and a median latency of
  27 s. It is a code-tuned model; the instruction-tuned `qwen3:4b` named in
  `docs/LOCAL_SETUP.md` was not installed and should be tried next.
- **Airavata** (the Indic LLM the proposal names) fails on two counts:
  - It rewrote over half of the dialogue's characters (rewrite CER 52%), which breaks golden
    rule 5.
  - Its summaries are generic ("A multi-speaker transcript from an authentic Indian audio
    recording"). Only 12% beat the null threshold, and only 9% retrieve their own transcript.
- **OpenHathi-base is a base model, not instruction-tuned.** Its usable outputs echo the input:
  rewrite CER 0, translation rate 0, and empty summaries, so grounding cannot be computed. It does
  not do the task.
- **Local 7B models on a 4 GB GPU spill to the CPU.** They take 27–33 s per transcript.
- **The project guardrail rejects good summaries** (`src/postprocessing/guardrails.py`,
  `check_factual_consistency`). It requires at least 25% lexical overlap of "key terms" between
  summary and transcript. An English summary of a Hindi transcript shares almost no surface words
  with it, so faithful summaries fail: 5.9% pass for Gemini. Two more bugs make it worse:
   - Its `\b[a-zA-Zऀ-ൿ]{3,}\b` term regex breaks words at matras, because `\b` treats
     combining marks as non-word characters.
   - It penalises every capitalised English word absent from the transcript, which is every
     proper noun written in English.

  OpenHathi "passes" at 100% only because it echoes the input. **Proposed fix (not yet applied):**
  replace the lexical test with the calibrated document-level LaBSE check used in this benchmark.
